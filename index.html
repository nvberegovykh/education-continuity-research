A critical review and research protocol for the 3–4–3–4–3–4–2 model

Version 1.0 · 2 November 2026 Originating concept: Nikita Beregovykh. Literature synthesis and protocol: AI-assisted editorial preparation at the concept author's request.

Publication status: Research proposal and targeted narrative review. Not peer reviewed. No participants recruited, no original outcome data collected, and no effectiveness or optimal-age result established. Cited organizations have not endorsed this model.

Abstract

This paper develops a proposed coordination system connecting early childcare, general education, practical projects, professional preparation, housing and healthcare access, and independent adult life. Its organizing sequence is seven stages lasting 3, 4, 3, 4, 3, 4, and 2 years. The central question is whether continuity of support and a gradual transfer of responsibility can reduce avoidable disruption while preserving educational breadth, privacy, and freedom to change direction. A targeted review compares the proposal with Finnish comprehensive education, Japan's school structure, Montessori developmental organization, Swiss vocational training, US Career Academies, project-based learning trials, and integrated student supports. These provide relevant precedents and mixed component evidence; none validates the entire sequence or a genetic timetable. The paper proposes a staged evaluation, independent assessment, portable employer partnerships, confidential developmental assistance, and an explicit termination of compulsory developmental monitoring at legal adulthood. Independent family planning is not conditional on graduation, income, test scores, or employer approval. Ages 27–30 are considered an optional recovery and reassessment window, with no claim that a universal retreat is biologically necessary.

1. Research question, scope, and method

The intended benefit is consistent coordination and stable progress as people grow older: a child should not lose assistance simply because childcare ends; a student should understand the connection between classroom knowledge and practical activity; and a young adult should have access to housing, healthcare, relationships education, and work preparation without surrendering control over private life.

The primary hypothesis is that coordinated handovers, appropriate material support, and gradually increasing responsibility improve learning and self-directed functioning compared with usual provision. A separate hypothesis concerns timing: does the exact seven-stage calendar add value beyond equally well-resourced support using existing school ages? These hypotheses must be tested separately. Better outcomes after adding services would not establish that the proposed birthdays caused the improvement.

This is a targeted narrative review, with searches and source checks conducted on 2 November 2026. Searches covered early development; national school structures; vocational and employer-linked education; project-based learning; integrated supports; educational genetics; social-emotional assessment; autonomy; and recovery from work. Sources were selected for direct relevance, identifiable methods or official institutional descriptions, and accessible supporting records. The review prioritizes original evaluations, primary research reports, official system descriptions, and international guidance. It includes contrary and null findings.

This is not an exhaustive database search, systematic review, meta-analysis, clinical guideline, or jurisdiction-specific legal opinion. No complete screening count, preregistered search, or formal risk-of-bias instrument is claimed. Some studies were inspected through their author/institutional summaries or abstracts; those access limits are recorded in the source register. Descriptions of a system establish what it does, not that it outperforms alternatives. Research evidence, institutional precedents, normative commitments, and new design choices are distinguished throughout.

2. Correcting the age arithmetic without losing the proposal

The proposed durations sum to 23 years: 3 + 4 + 3 + 4 + 3 + 4 + 2 = 23. For a continuous calendar beginning at birth, the boundaries are therefore birthdays 0, 3, 7, 10, 14, 17, 21, and 23. In this paper, “3 to under 7” means the third birthday up to, but not including, the seventh birthday.

The initiating description instead labels the stages “0–3, 4–7, 8–10, 11–14, 15–17, 18–21, 22–23.” Read as completed ages, that first band lasts four years and the full calendar ends on the 24th birthday. These are two different operationalizations. The exact-duration model below preserves the numeric sequence; an alternative preserving the original completed-age labels must be evaluated separately and must not be described as an exact 23-year schedule.

The proposed review birthdays 0, 3, 7, 10, 14, 17, and 21 are retained as support handovers, with a closing review offered at 23. Birth is a family and health support review, not an IQ test. Stage dates organize provision; they do not declare that a person has become mature or immature overnight. The age of legal adulthood remains independent of this calendar. A reference implementation may use 18, but each participating jurisdiction must specify its actual rules before enrolling anyone.

All seven themes overlap. Infants communicate; young children cooperate; adolescents need play and care; adults continue learning coordination, patience, and responsibility. These are changing emphases, not exclusive developmental compartments.

3. The seven-stage service and learning model

Stage 1 · Birth to under 3 · Body, attachment, and early communication · 3 years

Offer responsive childcare, caregiver support, safe movement and play, language-rich interaction, nutrition and healthcare access, and early assistance when concerns arise. Stable relationships matter more than training children to comply. This is consistent with the WHO/UNICEF/World Bank nurturing-care framework's emphasis on health, nutrition, responsive caregiving, safety, and early learning S01.

Observation should support the child and caregiver. It must not assign a fixed identity, genetic destiny, employability class, or lifelong score. Developmental concerns should receive help when noticed rather than waiting until a stage boundary. AAP guidance includes validated developmental screening at 9, 18, and 30 months and autism screening at 18 and 24 months; this illustrates why sparse birthday checkpoints cannot replace routine care S02.

Stage 2 · 3 to under 7 · Communication and supported coexistence · 4 years

Provide play, language, early literacy and numeracy, art, physical activity, conflict repair, consent and body boundaries, accessible communication, and everyday group routines. A specialized facility can offer safe miniature shops, gardens, workshops, shared meals, and role play, connected to ordinary community life. It should supplement family and community relationships rather than create an institutionally isolated social world.

The transition product is a descriptive learning and support plan: what the child enjoys, how the child communicates, which accommodations work, and which assistance needs to continue. It is not a pass/fail judgment about social conformity. Children with disabilities, different home languages, or atypical development retain equal access and may need individualized pacing.

Stage 3 · 7 to under 10 · Guided projects and shared inquiry · 3 years

Introduce scientific, artistic, sporting, and civic environments through concrete group projects. A museum, cooperative, local company, public service, or community group can supply a meaningful brief. For example, a class might design an accessible planter, measure its water use, explain the results, and present alternatives within an agreed material budget.

The teacher prepares a roadmap with questions, subject goals, safety points, and fallback options. Pupils choose among feasible approaches and share roles. Each project moves through understanding the request, planning, making, checking, presenting, and reflecting. Explicit instruction in reading, mathematics, science, and other subjects remains protected; a group product cannot reveal every child's knowledge.

This adapts the proposed external-investor relationship into an educational partnership. The partner can fund materials and give feedback. School educators retain responsibility for curriculum, access, assessment, and the child's welfare. A sponsor does not own a class or acquire a claim on a child's future labor.

Stage 4 · 10 to under 14 · Persistence, social judgment, and dependable practice · 4 years

Develop sustained attention, revision, negotiation, feedback, and contribution to longer projects. “Learning patience” means tolerating uncertainty and working through difficulty with support; it must not mean enduring mistreatment or accepting authority without question. Teach local conventions alongside the ability to examine them, disagree respectfully, and recognize discrimination.

Rotate projects across science, arts, sports, public life, and different occupations. Provide individual teaching and assessment as well as team activity. Preserve room for interests with no immediate commercial customer. Review progress regularly enough to act on emerging needs, without expanding into continuous behavioral scoring.

Stage 5 · 14 to under 17 · Responsibility and increasing project autonomy · 3 years

Students form teams, set strategies, negotiate deadlines, test ideas, document failures, and propose efficiency improvements. Low-risk planning can occur without continuous teacher observation. Teachers remain available for advice and assess declared milestones, individual knowledge, and ethical practice.

“Unsupervised” must be defined by activity. Private brainstorming or an ordinary design task can be independent. Hazardous laboratory work, unsafe equipment, fieldwork, and legally restricted employment still require appropriate safeguards. An authentic deadline does not justify avoidable injury, exploitation, or punitive public exposure of mistakes.

The proposed employer examination becomes an independently moderated transition portfolio, including subject learning, project evidence, reflection, and several route options. A company may assess suitability for its own opportunity, but must not control access to all education or pronounce on a student's overall social worth. An appeal, alternative assessor, and route to another sponsor are essential design conditions.

Stage 6 · 17 to under 21 · Professional preparation and economic capability · 4 years

Offer university, vocational college, apprenticeships, mixed study/work routes, entrepreneurship education, and bridge courses. Four years is a planning allowance for this model, not a claim that all qualifications should last four years. Local accreditation and entry ages govern actual programs.

Learning includes professional competence, employment rights, contracts, budgeting, taxes and benefits, consumer protection, housing options, healthcare navigation, hobbies, relationships, and caring responsibilities. Discuss earned income and the concepts, costs, uncertainty, and unequal access associated with asset-based income. Passive income is not a required achievement or a guaranteed outcome; people should not need speculative investments or family wealth to graduate.

Support practical independence as early as the person chooses and the law permits. The stage includes both minors and adults in a reference system with adulthood at 18, so consent and control of information must change during it. A course calendar cannot extend parental or institutional authority over an adult.

Stage 7 · 21 to under 23 · Paid work and learning from recoverable mistakes · 2 years

Offer a paid placement or ordinary employment with a trained mentor, transparent expectations, appropriate insurance and social protection, time for learning, and a way to change employer. Interpret “expediting” as timely access to guidance and help resolving obstacles, not an obligation to work faster at the expense of safety.

The focus is judgment under real constraints: estimating cost, admitting uncertainty, repairing errors, communicating delays, and improving a process. Do not manufacture harmful experiences to teach the “cost of mistakes.” Errors should be made recoverable where possible; exposure to real work does not remove an employer's duties. The ILO's quality-apprenticeship guidance provides a relevant framework for structured training, remuneration, protection, and inclusion S17.

At 23, offer a voluntary closing handover of credentials, selected work, service contacts, and records the person wants to keep. Adults may live independently earlier, continue study, care for others, or need continuing support. Completion does not certify a permanently finished adult personality.

4. Comparison with existing systems and interventions

Finland: comprehensive schooling and welfare around learning

Finnish basic education ordinarily begins in the year a child turns seven and lasts nine years. Official descriptions include free learning materials, meals, health and welfare services, and transport under specified conditions S03. This is a precedent for treating the conditions of learning as part of educational provision. The proposed model extends coordination into housing and adult transitions and divides stages differently. Finland's structure does not prove those additional components or birthdays are optimal.

Japan: a contrasting calendar and a stable class structure

NIER's 2011 overview describes a 6–3–3–4 structure and classroom teachers who generally teach primary subjects, with specialist and team-teaching arrangements S04. This offers a comparison for a continuing class teacher and a different division of school years. The historical source is used for structure, not current enrollment statistics or a claim that school organization expresses inherited human stages. The proposal's 3–4–3 school segment would require a separate curriculum and qualification mapping.

Montessori: prepared environments and multi-age organization

The American Montessori Society describes four broad developmental planes, 0–6, 6–12, 12–18, and 18–24, with multi-age subgroups and changing educational environments S05. These resemble the proposal's interest in coordinated environments and increasing independence. They use a different rhythm. A pedagogical developmental theory is not genetic confirmation of either timetable, and descriptions of Montessori practice are not an effectiveness trial.

Switzerland: school and paid workplace training

Swiss vocational education combines classroom learning with paid workplace training, sometimes supplemented by training centers, and allows further qualifications S06. This is a relevant precedent for employer participation and pathways that remain open. The proposal should borrow structured shared responsibility and portability rather than lock a child to one firm. National labor-market outcomes cannot isolate the effect of the training calendar from other institutions and economic conditions.

US Career Academies: relevant causal evidence, limited generalization

MDRC's randomized evaluation across nine high schools found average annual earnings gains of 11 percent over eight years after expected graduation, with stronger gains among young men. The study did not establish a broad increase in educational attainment S07. This supports investigating combined academic, career, and employer-linked provision. It does not test early-childhood contracting, the seven-stage calendar, or guaranteed results for every subgroup.

Project-based learning: implementation and outcomes matter

The Knowledge in Action evaluation examined a supported project curriculum in AP US Government and AP Environmental Science. Its first-year randomized comparison found an approximately eight-percentage-point increase in credit-qualifying AP scores. The intervention included curriculum materials and substantial teacher development S08. This is evidence for that instructional package in that context, not for removing teachers or replacing all subjects with customer work.

By contrast, the EEF trial of Learning through REAL Projects found no clear positive effect on literacy or school engagement; substantial attrition and crossover weakened confidence. Qualitative reports identified possible communication and teamwork benefits S09. The implication is to preserve subject instruction, fund teacher preparation, and measure individual learning rather than assume that making a product proves educational success.

Integrated student supports: plausible coordination, mixed evidence

A 2025 Tennessee community-school evaluation reported improvements in some language-arts and school-stability outcomes, no change in several other academic outcomes, and increased chronic absenteeism. It used changes in school status over time rather than random assignment S10. This makes service coordination worth testing while warning against assuming that every bundle of services will help. The proposed housing, healthcare, and adult-transition services need their own access, cost, and outcome evaluation.

5. Genetics and development: what the evidence can support

The initiating observation refers to “genetical tendencies.” This review found no direct evidence establishing the exact 3–4–3–4–3–4–2 rhythm as a genetically programmed progression. Absence of such evidence in this targeted review is not an exhaustive proof of absence; it is a reason not to advertise a biological discovery.

Okbay and colleagues' educational-attainment study found population-level polygenic prediction, with associations substantially reduced when direct effects were separated from family-related influences S11. It studied educational attainment, not an optimal age for childcare transitions, career choice, independence, or family formation. Heritability or prediction within a population does not make an outcome fixed for an individual. This proposal therefore collects no DNA and uses no genetic score for placement, housing, employment, partner matching, or access to care.

NIMH describes important developmental changes continuing into the mid-to-late twenties S13. That broad account does not identify a single birthday that establishes competence, legal adulthood, reproductive readiness, or the need for a retreat. The model should adapt support to the person and task, preserving legal rights while offering assistance.

6. Assessment at 0, 3, 7, 10, 14, 17, and 21

Replace a compulsory repeated “IQ, EQ, and identity test” with a confidential developmental and educational support review. The purpose is to notice needs, discuss progress, and coordinate assistance. A test without funded follow-up is an inadequate intervention.

  • At birth: offer caregiver and health-service coordination. Do not administer an IQ, EQ, or identity classification.
  • At 3: discuss language, movement, play, communication access, caregiver observations, and any clinical referrals. Routine early screening continues between handovers.
  • At 7: review school access, foundational learning, hearing/vision concerns referred to appropriate services, communication, peer experience, and accommodations.
  • At 10: review individual subject learning, group participation, confidence, interests, and the quality of the learning environment.
  • At 14: review workload, learning, safety, emerging goals, support preferences, and the transfer of more decisions to the student.
  • At 17: discuss several next routes, qualification requirements, funding, housing/healthcare access, and a clear explanation of rights at adulthood.
  • At 21: offer an adult-led review of professional preparation, work conditions, wellbeing, and any requested support. Participation and onward sharing are voluntary.

Where a qualified professional identifies a valid reason for cognitive assessment, use an appropriate standardized instrument, accommodations, informed consent, and an explanation of uncertainty. A single score must not determine a life route. The program should not create its own unvalidated “EQ index.” OECD work notes limitations in the comparability and interpretation of self-reported social-emotional assessments S12.

Identity exploration should be self-described and optional. No assessment should determine which personality, religion, gender, sexual orientation, or lawful political belief is acceptable. “Psychological stability” here means accessible support for distress and functioning, not the suppression of difference. Teachers can raise concerns; clinical diagnosis belongs to appropriately qualified professionals. Additional assistance is available when needed, without waiting for an official checkpoint.

7. Support services and a ten-year partnership without dependency

One named coordinator can help connect services, but the coordinator should not become a universal observer. Education, clinical care, housing, employment, and optional adult social services need distinct purposes and access controls. A referral can transmit a requested accommodation or contact preference without transmitting an entire psychological history.

Housing support should offer safe options, tenancy information, transport planning, and emergency pathways. Healthcare coordination should support continuity between child and adult services. Neither should disappear solely because a learner changes sponsor, declines optional assessment, leaves a relationship, or takes longer to qualify. These are proposed protections and resource commitments; the present paper supplies no funded housing guarantee.

The suggested ten-year company contract should be a commitment by a sponsor consortium to support education, with annual review, replacement funding arrangements, and learner transfer rights. It must not be a child's advance employment contract, debt obligation, restrictive covenant, or agreement to supply commercially necessary labor. Diversify sponsors, include public and nonprofit briefs, and provide a public fallback if a company fails. A school, rather than an investor alone, determines class composition using published educational and inclusion criteria.

Partners may state a product request, resource constraints, and quality criteria. Independent educators determine whether the brief is age-appropriate, which knowledge it teaches, and how assessment works. Commercially valuable work needs a clear rights and compensation policy before it begins; students need a fair choice about public attribution and portfolio use. Apprenticeship protections should follow applicable law and established quality principles S17.

A proposed dating service becomes optional adult social introduction, separate from assessment, employment, housing, and healthcare. It has no minor matchmaking, genetic matching, ranking of reproductive suitability, required participation, or employer access to private profiles. Consent, accessible opportunities to meet people, and a safe way to decline are its only educationally relevant purposes. This is a design option with no efficacy evidence established here.

8. Explicit autonomy and surveillance-termination declaration

Privacy applies from childhood. The Convention on the Rights of the Child provides a relevant international framework for privacy, a child's views, educational development, and protection from harmful work; domestic implementation and legal duties differ S15. Necessary safeguarding is different from recording private life for institutional convenience.

This proposal rejects continuous monitoring of private conversations, dating, intimate behavior, home activity, emotional biometrics, or location outside a specific justified safety purpose. It does not institute genetic surveillance or automated identity scoring at any age. Safety equipment or attendance records need a defined purpose, restricted access, and a stated retention period; they must not silently become a lifelong behavioral dossier.

The following timings are proposed policy defaults, not research-proven optimal ages:

  • Before 14: supervision is proportionate to the activity, with increasing private space and participation in decisions. Share only what is needed for care, learning, or an applicable safeguarding duty.
  • From 14: end continuous observation of ordinary low-risk project strategy. Use agreed milestones, accessible advice, and activity-specific safety oversight. The original completed-age variant places this school transition at 15; the rights principles do not depend on that one-year choice.
  • At legal adulthood, using 18 in the reference implementation: compulsory program-level developmental tracking and automatic parental/employer access terminate. A student does not have to wait until 21, 23, 24, or 30. Any legally required records or specific safeguarding duties must be explained individually; they do not justify general surveillance. Supported decision-making should remain available without assuming incapacity.
  • After adulthood: optional support and research follow-up require separate, specific, revocable consent. Offer a maximum 12-month authorization before renewal as a proposed operating rule. Silence is not renewal. Declining follow-up must not affect essential services, qualifications, employment, or family choices.
  • At 23 in the exact-duration model, or 24 in the completed-age alternative: offer administrative closure, a usable copy of selected records, a list of records retained and why, withdrawal from optional tracking, and deletion or de-identification where permitted. Adult control already began earlier.
  • At 27–30: any retreat or review is initiated by the adult or accepted through a fresh invitation. It never reactivates former school monitoring or parental access.

Ordinary lawful workplace management, clinical records, and course administration remain governed by their own limited purposes and local requirements. They must not be pooled into a program-wide adult surveillance system. A closure procedure should verify access removal, data-sharing cessation, and completion of promised deletion, with an independent complaint route.

9. Independent family planning: support without institutional permission

Family planning is independent of the stage calendar. WHO states that people have the right to choose whether to have children and to decide their number, spacing, and timing S14. No sponsor, teacher, housing provider, or program psychologist may require a readiness certificate, minimum IQ/EQ, income level, relationship match, graduation, or completion of work experience before respecting an adult's choices.

Offer age-appropriate education about bodies, boundaries, consent, relationships, and reproductive health before adult decisions arise. UNESCO's guidance places this learning within human rights and the young person's interests S16. Adolescents should receive appropriate confidential care and support within local law; the program must explain actual confidentiality limits rather than promise unrestricted secrecy.

The optimized service timing, proposed for evaluation, is preparation before school exit, optional practical planning during ages 17–23, and another optional opportunity at 27–30 or whenever requested. Discuss childcare, housing, caring responsibilities, employment flexibility, and access to qualified reproductive-health advice. These are opportunities to obtain help, not preferred ages for conception, marriage, or parenthood. Clinical and personal circumstances differ; this review establishes no universal optimal age for pregnancy. Choosing no children, a later family, an earlier family, or another lawful household structure remains compatible with successful participation.

10. Ages 23–27 and a voluntary retreat window at 27–30

The model needs a bridge between initial work experience and the proposed retreat. During ages 23–27, keep qualifications and records portable, allow re-entry into education, and make advice available on request. People may change jobs, study, provide care, move, or build a household. BLS longitudinal reporting shows substantial early-career job mobility; it is descriptive cohort evidence, not a prediction of any participant's career S20.

For ages 27–30, investigate an optional opportunity for recovery, reflection, retraining, health consultation, relationship discussion, and review of work and housing arrangements. “Retreat” should mean a protected choice to step back, not social withdrawal, mandatory unemployment, institutional residence, or a three-year pause imposed on everyone. Similar opportunities should remain available at other ages, especially when stress or a major transition occurs.

Vacation evidence supports examining recovery but does not identify this age window. A 2025 meta-analysis of 32 studies found wellbeing benefits and a more sustained pattern than earlier reviews had suggested S19. Vacation studies cannot establish that a longer developmental retreat at 27–30 improves life outcomes. WHO workplace guidance also addresses organizational conditions and return-to-work support, indicating that time away should not substitute for improving harmful work conditions S18.

Proposed options for a pilot are a protected 2–6-week leave, a reduced-load period, or a separately funded 6–12-month retraining/care break where desired. These durations are design choices, not clinical prescriptions or established optima. Confirm an income plan, continuity of housing and healthcare, caring arrangements, the person's preferred level of contact, and a flexible return route. Employers receive only the information needed to administer leave; private reflection and clinical discussions remain private. Advice should be adapted to circumstances and may include remaining in ordinary work if that is the person's preference.

The retreat study should compare funded options with usual leave and compare similar opportunities at other adult ages. Measure recovery, autonomy, job quality, learning, financial strain, and return experience. A biological claim about 27–30 would require direct evidence of age-specific effects, not simply observing that participants enjoyed a break.

11. A testable evaluation program

Phase A: co-design and feasibility

Before delivery, specify a jurisdiction, responsible provider, funding, school and qualification equivalence, safeguarding responsibilities, staff capacity, accessibility, and each service's legal basis. Consult learners, caregivers, educators, clinicians, employers, disability advocates, and independent privacy and research-ethics reviewers. An ethics decision for actual research is still required; publication of this proposal does not grant permission to study children.

Start with a limited transition cohort, rather than replacing a whole birth-to-adulthood system at once. Publish recruitment, consent materials, eligibility, a comparison plan, measures, and analysis choices before accessing outcomes. Record adaptations and implementation costs. Participation in research must be separable from receiving ordinary services.

Phase B: distinguish coordination from the calendar

For an initial school transition, consider a three-arm cluster-randomized design if feasible: (A) usual provision; (B) enhanced coordination and projects on the existing calendar; (C) the same resources and staff development organized around the proposed stage boundaries. Randomize schools or service areas to reduce spillover, stratify by relevant baseline conditions, and use independent outcome assessment where possible.

Compare B with A to estimate the added package under those conditions. Compare C with B to estimate the incremental calendar effect. If resources, instructional hours, or selection rules differ between B and C, timing cannot be isolated. Where changing school ages is legally or practically impossible, study handover timing only and state that the whole calendar remains untested. A phased implementation without randomization needs stronger adjustment and explicit residual-confounding limits.

Select one primary educational outcome and one primary autonomy/wellbeing outcome for the specific cohort, with validated measures appropriate to language, age, and disability. Report subject attainment, engagement, distress, workload, service access, exclusion, injuries, and unwanted monitoring as secondary or harm outcomes. At work transitions add employment quality, net earnings, retention, further education, and freedom to change employer. Marriage, childbirth, personality conformity, and genetic scores are not success targets.

Phase C: design, analysis, and adverse outcomes

Calculate sample size before recruitment using a meaningful target effect, expected cluster correlation, cluster size variation, attrition, and multiple-primary-outcome plan. No sample size is asserted in this paper because those inputs are not yet available. Analyze assignment on an intention-to-treat basis, account for clustering, report confidence intervals and absolute differences, and prespecify handling of missing outcomes. Report departures, crossovers, and differential loss to follow-up.

Assess access and outcomes across socioeconomic circumstances, disability, language, sex/gender where appropriate and consented, and other locally relevant equity dimensions. Protect small groups from identification and avoid unsupported subgroup conclusions. Track whether employer selection creates apparent gains by excluding students who need more support. Publish null results, increased workload, displacement of foundational teaching, privacy complaints, and service interruption as prominently as improvements.

Independent oversight should pause recruitment or the relevant activity after a serious safeguarding breach, coerced participation, systematic denial of essential services, or a material data breach, with a response process defined before the pilot. Numerical stopping thresholds for educational harm require a justified statistical and ethical plan; none has been invented here. Safety action should not wait for a statistically significant effect.

Phase D: long-term follow-up and retreat evaluation

Use multiple age cohorts to learn about different transitions sooner, while recognizing that this cannot recreate a single birth-to-30 experience. A true longitudinal evaluation takes decades. Re-consent at adulthood and whenever the purpose materially changes. Optional adult follow-up must survive withdrawal without penalty through methods that acknowledge missingness rather than forcing observation.

Evaluate the retreat separately, preferably randomizing the offer of funded leave or comparing phased access where ethical and practical. Prespecify follow-up immediately after return, at 3 months, and at 12 months as proposed research intervals. Record age continuously and test any claimed 27–30 advantage against comparable adults outside that range. Caregiving, income security, job conditions, and self-selection are plausible alternative explanations.

Costs and the meaning of optimization

Record staffing, training, facilities, transport, assessment, healthcare coordination, housing support, paid placement support, leave funding, administration, and independent oversight. Distinguish transfers such as allowances from real resource costs and state the evaluation perspective. Include opportunity costs for learners and caregivers. No national budget, guaranteed return, or cost-saving estimate is available.

“Optimized timing” should mean the best observed balance of learning, wellbeing, access, autonomy, and cost within explicit rights constraints. It cannot mean maximum employer output or minimum monitoring expense. No single composite life score should rank people. Until comparative results exist, the calendar, review intervals, and retreat durations remain provisional service-design hypotheses.

12. Research conclusions and immediate next step

The proposal's strongest testable idea is continuity: connect care, education, practical learning, and adult services so that support can continue while authority over the person's life decreases. Existing systems offer useful components, and evaluations show that implementation quality and context matter. There is no established evidence that the exact seven-stage duration pattern is genetically determined, globally optimal, or sufficient to ensure psychological or economic stability.

Proceed by developing one locally lawful, funded transition pilot with independent evaluation and a genuinely equivalent comparison group. Preserve teacher guidance, broad education, sponsor transfer rights, confidential assistance, and adult freedom to disengage. Compulsory developmental monitoring ends at legal adulthood in the proposed policy; later reviews are voluntary. Independent family planning is never postponed by this educational calendar. The 27–30 retreat remains an optional, separately testable service, available without reviving surveillance.

Evidence still required: original comparative data on learning and autonomy; effects of the calendar beyond resources; valid support-review methods; feasibility and costs of integrated services; durability of benefits; equitable sponsor participation; and age-specific evidence for any retreat advantage. Publication is the beginning of research scrutiny, not confirmation of the model.

Planes of Development and Sensitive Periods: Foundations of the Montessori Multi-Age Classroom.

S07 · James J. Kemple, MDRC (2008)

Type: randomized program evaluation. Access: evaluator's report overview and key findings; educational-attainment finding cross-checked against US Department of Labor study summary.

Type: quasi-experimental statewide evaluation. Access: author's university research-alliance abstract and findings.
